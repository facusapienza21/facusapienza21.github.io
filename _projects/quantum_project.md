During my physics degree at the University of Buenos Aires, I worked on the resource theory of quantum thermodynamics under the supervision of Augusto Roncaglia, in collaboration with [Federico Cerisola](https://df.uba.ar/en/component/researchers/member/91-Federico_Cerisola).

Classical thermodynamics describes systems with a huge number of particles, where fluctuations average out and quantities like free energy tell us exactly how much work a process costs or produces.
At the nanoscale, this is no longer true: systems are small, processes may happen only once, and fluctuations matter.
The resource theory approach treats thermodynamics as a set of allowed operations (exchanging energy with a thermal bath while conserving the total energy) and asks which state transformations are possible and at what cost.
In this *single-shot* regime, the standard laws of thermodynamics are replaced by a family of constraints that are more restrictive than the usual free energy.

### Correlations as a resource

Correlations between physical systems are a valuable resource in quantum information, and creating them usually has an energetic cost.
We studied their role in the thermodynamic process of *state formation*, that is, the minimum work needed to prepare a given state from thermal equilibrium.
We found that correlations can also be a thermodynamic resource: the cost of preparing many copies of a state is reduced if the copies are allowed to be correlated.
We computed this minimum cost for any finite number of subsystems and showed that, by allowing an amount of correlations that grows only logarithmically with the number of subsystems, the standard result where free energy quantifies the cost is recovered in the asymptotic limit.
This work was the topic of my physics thesis and was published in *Nature Communications*.

### Heat engines in the nanoregime

We then applied these ideas to heat engines operating in the single-shot regime and extracting a finite amount of *deterministic* work, that is, work without fluctuations.
We showed that the efficiency of these engines is strictly smaller than Carnot's efficiency, due to a fundamental irreversibility in single-shot transformations.
When fluctuations in the extracted work are allowed, there is a trade-off: larger fluctuations give higher efficiency.
Finally, when the working medium is made of many particles, creating correlations between them that scale logarithmically with their number allows the engine to approach Carnot's efficiency while still extracting deterministic work.
